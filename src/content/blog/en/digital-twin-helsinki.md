## Why accurate as-built records matter

Once a metro line finishes construction and moves into operation, its as-built records become the most important reference document for the infrastructure operator across a lifecycle that often spans 50 to 100 years. The real-world position of tunnel lining, utility ducts, power cables, signaling equipment, and M&E systems rarely matches the original design drawings exactly, since adjustments inevitably occur during construction. When as-built records exist only as paper drawings or scattered CAD files, looking anything up during a repair, retrofit, or incident response becomes slow and error-prone. Finland, with its long tradition of developing open BIM standards through organizations such as buildingSMART, is one of the places that adopted a comprehensive digitized approach to underground infrastructure records early on, including for the Helsinki Metro.

## From paper records to a digital twin

A digital twin is a three-dimensional digital model of the as-built asset that is continuously updated to reflect its real, current condition — distinct from a design-stage BIM model, which only describes original intent before construction. Building a digital twin for a metro line requires integrating as-built data from several sources into one unified model:

- Design and construction BIM models updated with the actual changes recorded during construction (as-built BIM).
- 3D laser scan data (point clouds) capturing the real geometry of tunnels, stations, and structures after completion, used to verify and correct the model.
- Digital records of M&E equipment, signaling systems, cables, and pipework, tagged with identifiers linked to each asset's maintenance history.
- Geotechnical and geological survey data along the alignment, used for long-term risk analysis of the underground structures.

## Benefits for operations and maintenance

Once a digital twin is kept up to date, the operator can instantly look up the precise location of any component or piece of equipment without trial excavation or digging through old paper drawings. Preventive maintenance planning becomes more accurate because each asset's installation history, specifications, and expected service life are linked directly to the model. When an incident occurs or a retrofit or extension is planned, engineers can simulate the intervention on the digital model before touching the physical asset, reducing the risk of clashing with existing infrastructure. A digital model also supports training for new operations staff, who can walk through the underground structure virtually without needing site access.

## Implementation challenges

Building and maintaining a digital twin for a metro system is far from simple. The biggest challenge is ensuring the quality and consistency of input data: if contractors fail to update as-built records promptly and accurately, the digital model quickly drifts from the real condition of the asset. Integrating data from multiple contractors and different software systems — structural, M&E, signaling — into an open format that multiple tools can read also requires strict standardization starting from the contract stage. Keeping the model aligned with reality after every repair or minor modification requires a data-update process built into day-to-day operating procedures, rather than a one-time exercise performed only at handover.

## Lessons for other projects

Finland's experience shows that the real value of a digital twin lies not in eye-catching 3D visualization, but in as-built data that is digitized, structured, and continuously updated starting from the construction phase, rather than reconstructed years after the asset is already in operation. For new metro projects, building open-BIM-based as-built handover requirements into construction contracts from the outset, along with a data change-management process that runs through the entire project, is the key preparation step for a digital twin that is actually useful for long-term operations.
