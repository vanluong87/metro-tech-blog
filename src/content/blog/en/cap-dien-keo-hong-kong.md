## Why metro systems need a dedicated traction power system

Electric metro trains need a continuous, stable power supply along the entire length of a line to run their traction motors. Unlike national railways, which typically use high-voltage AC, most urban metro lines run on lower-voltage DC power — commonly in the 600-750V DC range — reflecting short station spacing, frequent acceleration and braking, and the need for compact equipment that fits inside tunnels or along elevated structures. There are two main ways to deliver this power to a train: a third rail running alongside the running rails, or an overhead catenary system (OCS) paired with a pantograph mounted on the train's roof.

## Third rail systems

A third rail is a conductive rail mounted parallel to the two running rails, typically to one side or between them. The train collects power through a sliding contact shoe that touches the third rail directly, while return current flows back through the running rails themselves. The main advantage is that no overhead support structure or wiring is needed, which reduces the tunnel height required — a meaningful cost saving on underground sections. The drawback is that the third rail carries dangerous voltage close to ground level, so it must be fitted with protective covers or guards, and isolated gaps are required at crossings, turnouts, and walkways to keep maintenance staff and passengers safe, especially during emergency evacuations.

## Overhead catenary systems (OCS)

OCS delivers power through a tensioned wire system suspended above the train, collected via a pantograph on the roof. This approach suits surface and elevated sections well, allows higher voltages (often AC) to reduce transmission losses over longer distances, and avoids the risk of low-level direct contact. In exchange, it requires a larger clearance envelope for the wires and support structures, which is why it is rarely used in tunnels with constrained cross-sections.

## How Hong Kong's MTR combines both approaches

The MTR network in Hong Kong is a clear example of running both technologies side by side, matched to the characteristics of each line. Most of MTR's traditional urban metro lines use a DC third-rail system, well suited to a dense underground network beneath the city center. Meanwhile, lines that originated as intercity or suburban railways later upgraded and integrated into the MTR system use an overhead catenary with higher AC voltage, better suited to the higher operating speeds and longer station spacing found in less urbanized areas. Running two technologies within one network requires careful planning of transition points, technology-specific training for operations and maintenance staff, and rolling stock designed to be compatible with the power system of the line it runs on.

## Traction substations and redundancy

Regardless of the technology chosen, power is delivered from traction substations located along the line, which draw high-voltage power from the public grid and step it down — and rectify it to DC, where applicable — before feeding the third rail or catenary. To prevent service disruption if a substation fails, systems are typically designed around N+1 redundancy, where neighboring substations can temporarily absorb the load of a failed substation's section. Return current flowing through the running rails is also monitored and electrically isolated from surrounding structures to limit stray current corrosion, which can otherwise damage reinforcement steel in nearby underground structures.

## Takeaways

Choosing between third rail and OCS is not purely an electrical engineering decision — it is closely tied to whether a section runs underground or at grade, the desired operating speed, and the historical origin of each part of the network. MTR's experience shows that a mature metro system can successfully run multiple traction power technologies at once, provided it maintains consistent engineering standards, specialized staff training, and safety measures tailored to each power delivery method.
