## Why rolling stock is its own project workstream

In most metro projects, rolling stock is procured and managed as a separate contract package from tunnel and station construction. The reason is the manufacturing cycle: from contract signing to the first train being ready for test running can take several years, so the package has to start early, running in parallel with structural construction, to be ready in time for integrated system testing before commercial operation. Germany's rail manufacturing industry, home to long-established builders like Siemens Mobility, is one of the world's most experienced rolling-stock supply chains, delivering trains and related systems to metro networks across Europe and beyond.

## From design to factory release

Manufacturing a metro train runs through several tightly controlled stages:

1. **Detailed engineering design**: based on the client's technical requirements (track gauge, axle load, operating speed, compatibility with the CBTC signaling system, and so on), the manufacturer develops the carbody, drivetrain, interior, and control system design.
2. **Carbody fabrication and assembly**: car bodies are typically built from aluminum alloy or stainless steel to save weight, welded and assembled on dedicated production lines inside the factory.
3. **Electrical and mechanical fit-out**: traction motors, braking systems, air conditioning, passenger information systems, and central control units are integrated into the finished carbody shell.
4. **Factory Acceptance Test (FAT)**: before leaving the factory, each train undergoes a series of static checks (electrical systems, safety, structural integrity) and dynamic runs on the factory's internal test track or a dedicated test facility, confirming it meets the contracted technical specifications before shipment.

## Transport: a logistics challenge of its own

Unlike most industrial equipment, a metro train is oversized, cannot move under its own power on ordinary roads, and often has to travel very long distances — sometimes across national borders or between continents. Common transport methods include:

- **Rail transport**: where gauge and existing national rail connections allow, train cars can be hauled by a locomotive over the existing rail network to a point near the site.
- **Sea transport**: for projects far from the manufacturing plant, cars are specially packaged for protection and shipped by dedicated vessel, then unloaded at the nearest port.
- **Oversized road transport**: the final leg from a port or rail terminus to the site or depot usually requires specialized modular trailers, which may need special-movement permits and a prior route survey (overpass clearance, bridge load limits, road curve radii).

The entire transport operation needs detailed planning in coordination with local traffic authorities, since moving even a single train car typically causes temporary traffic disruption along its route.

## Receiving and integration at the depot

Once a train reaches the site, it enters the depot (the maintenance and storage facility) via a dedicated access track, where it goes through a site acceptance test, reassembly of any components removed for transport (such as pantographs or certain roof-mounted equipment), and static testing at the depot before being cleared to run on the main line for dynamic testing together with the signaling, power supply, and other line-wide systems.

## Takeaways

Experience from Germany's rail manufacturing industry shows that the success of the rolling stock package depends heavily on synchronizing manufacturing schedules with depot and mainline construction progress — if the depot isn't ready when the first train arrives on site, unnecessary storage costs and delays follow. Detailed transport logistics planning, with early involvement from local transport infrastructure authorities, should be treated as an inseparable part of the rolling stock contract, not merely an internal matter for the manufacturer.
