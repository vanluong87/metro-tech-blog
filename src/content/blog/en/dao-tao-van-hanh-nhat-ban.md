## Why staff training is a project workstream, not an afterthought

A new metro line, however well designed and built, cannot run safely without a workforce capable of operating, monitoring, and maintaining it every single day. Unlike many engineering tasks that can be accelerated by adding people or equipment, training people takes a fixed amount of time — a train operator or a signaling technician cannot simply "work faster" to compress the real-world experience that competence requires. For this reason, serious metro projects build staff training into the overall project schedule from an early stage, running in parallel with construction and commissioning, rather than waiting until the system is ready before starting to recruit and train people.

## Typical training levels

Metro operations and maintenance staff are usually trained in tiers that match their level of responsibility:

1. **Induction training**: general railway safety knowledge, internal procedures, organizational structure, and the operator's safety culture — required of every new employee regardless of their eventual role.
2. **Role-specific technical training**: train operators learn to drive trains and handle abnormal situations; controllers learn to monitor and coordinate traffic across the whole line; technicians learn to maintain individual subsystems (rolling stock, signaling, track, traction power, station electromechanical equipment).
3. **Simulator training**: operators and controllers practice on simulators that replicate a train cab or a central control room, allowing them to rehearse dangerous or rare scenarios — loss of signal, an on-board fire, a simulated derailment — that cannot safely be practiced on the real system.
4. **Supervised practice on the live system**: after classroom and simulator training, trainees get hands-on practice on the actual line — typically during the pre-opening testing phase — under the supervision of an experienced instructor, before being cleared to work independently.
5. **Competency assessment & professional certification**: trainees must pass theoretical and practical assessments to obtain a professional license (for example, a train driving license), usually issued by a railway safety regulator or an authorized body, valid for a limited period and subject to periodic reassessment.
6. **Refresher training**: once in revenue service, staff continue to receive periodic refresher training to stay current with updated procedures, rehearse abnormal-situation response, and maintain their certification.

## The role of simulation in modern training

One of the major shifts in metro staff training over recent decades has been the growing use of simulation equipment. Modern train-driving simulators can replicate control interfaces, physical feedback, and emergency scenarios with a high degree of fidelity — situations an operator will rarely encounter in real life but must be able to handle correctly the very first time. Repeated simulator practice builds the reflexes needed to respond to these situations without real-world risk, while also reducing the number of hours trainees need on the live, operating system — a resource that is always scarce, since it competes directly with the commercial train schedule.

## Japan's approach

Railway operators in Japan, from urban subway lines to intercity railways, are well known for a disciplined training culture that often spans several years before an employee is entrusted with a fully independent role, particularly as a train operator. The typical career path begins with support roles at stations or depots, building real operational experience over several years, before progressing to in-depth training to become a certified train operator or maintenance technician. This approach reflects a widely held view in Japan's railway industry: the ability to respond calmly and correctly to abnormal situations can only be built through accumulated real-world experience combined with ongoing training, and cannot be fully substituted by compressed training in the run-up to opening day.

## Takeaways

Operations and maintenance training is often undervalued in the overall planning of a new metro project, because it does not produce a tangible deliverable the way civil works or installed equipment do. International experience, however, shows that a large share of operational incidents in the first years of a new metro line's service trace back to staff inexperience in handling situations, rather than to technical faults in the system itself. Allocating sufficient time and resources to training — starting early, structured clearly across levels, and sustained continuously after opening — is therefore just as important as any other engineering workstream for long-term operational safety.
