## What is pipe jacking?

Pipe jacking — called microtunneling when the pipe diameter is small — is a trenchless method for installing pipes or culverts by using hydraulic jacks to push successive precast pipe segments (typically reinforced concrete or steel) from a jacking shaft through the ground to a reception shaft, while a small cutting head at the leading pipe excavates the soil ahead. Unlike a TBM used for a passenger-carrying main tunnel, pipe jacking is typically used for small to medium diameters (a few hundred millimeters to a few meters), making it well suited for drainage culverts, water supply pipes, power and telecom conduits, or utility ducts running along or crossing a metro corridor.

## Why it matters for metro projects

When building a new metro line, existing utilities — drainage, water pipes, electrical cables — often need to be relocated or newly installed to avoid conflicting with the tunnel and underground stations. In dense cities such as Tehran, open-cut excavation for these utility lines causes prolonged traffic disruption and directly affects residents' daily life. Pipe jacking allows these underground conduits to be installed beneath roads and existing structures with almost no open-cut excavation, significantly reducing the time public space is occupied by construction.

## Basic construction sequence

1. **Build the jacking and reception shafts**: Two vertical shafts are excavated and properly shored — one houses the hydraulic jacking system and a thrust wall, while the other receives the cutting head or serves as a connection point for the next drive.
2. **Install the jacking frame and cutting head**: The cutting head (which can be a hand-excavated type for small pipes or a mechanized head for microtunneling) is lowered into the jacking shaft and connected to the first pipe segment.
3. **Jack each pipe segment forward**: The hydraulic jacks push the cutting head and pipe toward the reception shaft; excavated spoil is transported back to the jacking shaft via conveyor, slurry pumping, or skip, depending on the technology used. After each jacking stroke, a new pipe segment is connected and the drive continues.
4. **Completion**: Once the cutting head reaches the reception shaft, it is recovered; joints between pipe segments are tested for watertightness before the system is put into service.

## Key technical factors to control

- **Line and grade accuracy**: Laser guidance or gyroscopic positioning systems keep the drive's deviation within allowable tolerances — critical when the pipeline runs close to existing foundations or crosses other underground utilities.
- **Jacking force and friction control**: As drive length increases, friction between the pipe and surrounding soil builds up; engineers typically install intermediate jacking stations or apply bentonite lubrication around the pipe to reduce resistance.
- **Face stability and groundwater control**: Depending on the geology, the appropriate cutting head type (open-face, earth pressure balance, or slurry) must be selected to avoid surface settlement or face instability when encountering water-bearing pockets or loose soils.

## Lessons for other cities

Experience from urban projects such as Tehran's metro shows that pipe jacking is an effective way to minimize traffic disruption and social impact when utilities must be built or relocated along a metro corridor. For cities expanding their metro networks where existing underground infrastructure is already dense, planning pipe-jacking drives early — in parallel with main tunnel design — helps avoid construction conflicts and optimizes the project's overall schedule.
