## The problem: public budgets can't fund every metro line

A metro line spanning several dozen kilometers, with underground stations and modern trains, typically costs billions of dollars to build. Meanwhile, public budgets in many large cities must also cover healthcare, education, housing, and other infrastructure, making it hard to dedicate the entire capital budget to a single metro project — especially when a city wants to build several lines in parallel to keep pace with urbanization. The public-private partnership (PPP) model emerged as a way to mobilize capital, technology, and operating expertise from the private sector, while the state retains its role in network planning and oversight of public service standards.

## How PPP works for metro projects

In principle, a PPP is a long-term contract (typically 20-35 years) between a public authority and a private consortium — usually combining a construction firm, an operator, and a financial investor — under which the parties share capital investment, risk, and returns according to agreed terms. Several specific structures exist:

- **DBFO (Design-Build-Finance-Operate)**: the private partner designs, builds, finances, and operates the line for the concession period, then hands it back to the state.
- **Operating concession**: the state funds the core infrastructure (tunnels, stations), while the private partner invests in trains and signaling and recovers its investment through fare revenue.
- **Availability-payment contracts**: the state makes periodic payments to the private partner based on the infrastructure's availability and service quality, separating ridership revenue risk from the private investor.

Each structure allocates risk differently — construction risk, ridership revenue risk, interest-rate and currency risk — and choosing the right one depends on the state's financial capacity and how confident forecasts of future travel demand are.

## What makes a metro PPP succeed

- **Clear risk allocation**: the contract must spell out which party bears which risk (construction delays, cost overruns, ridership below forecast), avoiding prolonged disputes when something goes wrong.
- **Fare-setting and oversight mechanisms**: since metro fares are often treated as a public good, the state typically needs a subsidy or minimum-revenue guarantee mechanism so the project remains attractive to private investors without pushing fares too high.
- **Long-term contract management capacity** within the public authority, since a PPP contract spanning decades requires a dedicated oversight body, not just a procurement team active only during the bidding phase.

## São Paulo's approach

Greater São Paulo is one of the most populous urban regions in South America, and the city has used PPP/concession arrangements for several newer metro and elevated rail lines, alongside lines built and operated by the traditional state-owned company. Under this approach, the state's public transport authority still handles network planning, sets the fare framework, and defines service standards, while the private consortium is responsible for raising construction capital, supplying trains, and running day-to-day operations for the length of the concession. This parallel approach — some lines funded directly by the state, others delivered through PPPs — has allowed São Paulo to expand its network faster than relying on public budgets alone, while keeping fare-setting unified across the whole system so passengers aren't charged differently depending on which line they ride.

## Takeaways

PPP is not a wholesale replacement for public investment, but a complementary tool that can speed up network expansion when public budgets are constrained. The experience of large cities like São Paulo shows that the real precondition for a successful metro PPP is a clear legal framework for risk allocation, a sound revenue-guarantee mechanism, and genuine long-term contract-management capacity within the public authority — not simply "handing the project over" to the private sector and hoping for the best.
