## Why handover deserves its own phase, not just a signature

After a new metro line finishes construction and technical trial running, it is tempting to picture the next step as simply cutting a ribbon and opening the doors to passengers. In practice, a carefully controlled handover and early commercial operation period sits between those two milestones, often lasting anywhere from a few weeks to several months. This is when legal and operational responsibility shifts from the construction contractor to the operating company, and when the system must run continuously with real passengers for the first time, rather than following a simulated schedule in a controlled environment. Skipping or compressing this phase meaningfully raises the risk of operational incidents in the first weeks of service — precisely the period when public and media attention is highest.

## The main steps in the handover process

Handing over a new metro line typically follows a sequence of steps:

1. **Trial running**: trains operate across the full line without passengers, checking how subsystems — signaling, traction power, rolling stock, ventilation, fire protection — interact under real operating conditions.
2. **Trial operation**: the system runs to the actual planned timetable over a sustained period (usually several weeks), closely replicating commercial service without carrying the general public, in order to surface problems that only appear during repeated, stable day-to-day running.
3. **Handover & acceptance**: a railway safety regulator or an authorized body conducts an overall acceptance review against the safety and technical criteria set at the start of the project, before issuing clearance for commercial operation. This is the pivotal step, as it formally transfers responsibility for safe operation from the contractor to the operator.
4. **Initial commercial operation**: the system opens to real passengers, typically at a lower train frequency than its final design capacity, under heightened monitoring.

## Initial commercial operation: a deliberately controlled start

One important but often overlooked feature is that train frequency in the first days and weeks after opening is usually well below the line's design capacity. This is not due to a shortage of trains — it is a deliberate choice. Lower frequency gives operations staff, especially train operators and controllers who still have limited hands-on experience with the new system, enough margin to handle abnormal situations without those issues cascading across the whole line. After a period of stable operation without significant incidents, frequency is increased in planned steps until the line reaches full design capacity. This "ramp-up" approach is common to nearly every newly opened metro line worldwide, though the exact pace of the ramp-up varies by system.

## Guangzhou's approach

Guangzhou has been one of the fastest and most continuously expanding metro networks in China over the past few decades, with new lines and extensions entering service almost every year. Opening so many lines in close succession pushed the operator to standardize handover and early-operation procedures into a repeatable process, rather than treating each line as an entirely separate undertaking. Lessons learned from earlier line openings shortened the trial operation period needed for later ones, while acceptance checklists, safety criteria, and frequency ramp-up procedures were gradually standardized across the network. This is a typical advantage enjoyed by cities running a long-term, continuous expansion program, compared with cities building only a single isolated line.

## Takeaways

The handover and initial commercial operation phase is often undervalued in overall project planning, since it comes after the "visible" construction work is finished and can be mistaken for a mere administrative formality. In reality, this is when the system faces sustained real-world operating load for the first time, and where most hidden design or construction issues — if any exist — tend to surface. Allowing enough time for trial operation, maintaining clear and transparent acceptance criteria, and accepting a lower-than-design frequency during the early period are all factors that help a new metro line settle into stable, safe commercial service from its very first days.
