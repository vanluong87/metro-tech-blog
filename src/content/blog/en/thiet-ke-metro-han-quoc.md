## Two design stages, two different goals

Once an alignment has been selected during the planning stage, a metro project moves into a sequence of engineering design work before construction can begin. That sequence is usually split into two main stages: **basic design** (also called preliminary design) and **detailed engineering design**. Each stage serves a different purpose and works at a different level of detail, but both feed into a design package reliable enough to support tendering and construction. Seoul's subway network — one of the largest and most efficiently operated metro systems in the world — offers a good example of how design is progressively refined through these stages.

## Basic design: setting the line's framework

Basic design turns the chosen alignment into the key technical parameters needed to secure investment approval and produce a more reliable cost estimate than was possible at the pre-feasibility stage. Its main components typically include:

- **Horizontal and vertical alignment**: fixing the centerline, curve radii, and gradients to match the train's design speed.
- **Station locations and sizing**: platform count and length sized for the longest trains expected, plus interchange points with bus routes or other metro lines.
- **Primary structure selection**: choosing between bored tunnel (TBM), cut-and-cover, or elevated viaduct, based on ground conditions, urban density, and available right-of-way.
- **Preliminary M&E systems**: rough sizing of substations, ventilation, and signaling systems — enough to plan space and cost, but well short of construction-ready drawings.

## Engineering design: working out every construction detail

Once basic design is approved, engineering (detailed) design turns each element into drawings precise enough to build from. This stage demands close coordination across many disciplines:

- **Structural design**: detailed calculations for tunnel linings, station structures, and foundations, accounting for seismic loads and in-service loads over the structure's design life.
- **Geotechnical design**: finalizing excavation support and ground treatment measures based on detailed site investigation data.
- **Track, signaling, and power systems**: laying out track geometry, train control systems, and traction power delivery at every specific point along the line.
- **Station architecture and fit-out**: arranging entrances, stairs, elevators, and platform screen doors — a feature widely deployed across Seoul's subway lines to improve passenger safety.
- **Fire and life safety**: sizing emergency egress routes and smoke ventilation systems for fire scenarios inside tunnels and underground stations.

## Lessons from Seoul's subway network

Decades of building out Seoul's subway network show the value of consistently applying a national urban railway design code across every stage, which keeps lines built by different consultants over different eras compatible with one another. Another notable practice is building passenger-safety requirements — such as full-height platform screen doors — into basic design from the start, rather than retrofitting them after a station is complete, which makes better use of architectural space and installation budget. Integrating station design with adjacent underground developments, such as shopping concourses or underground parking, as early as the engineering design stage is also common in dense cities like Seoul, helping make efficient use of scarce underground space.

## Common challenges

- **Multidisciplinary coordination**: engineering design requires structural, geotechnical, M&E, and architectural work to line up precisely — small mismatches between disciplines can cause space conflicts once construction begins.
- **Design changes between stages**: findings from detailed geotechnical investigation or real-world site constraints can force changes to an already-approved basic design, triggering additional approval procedures.
- **Balancing detail against schedule**: more detailed engineering design reduces construction risk, but also demands more time and consultant resources.

## Conclusion

Basic design and engineering design are two inseparable links in a metro project's lifecycle: one shapes the overall scheme for investment approval, the other works out every detail needed for safe, standards-compliant construction. South Korea's experience shows that building safety requirements and urban integration into the design process early, combined with consistent adherence to a unified design code, is key to keeping a large metro network coherent across many generations of projects.
