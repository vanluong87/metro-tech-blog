## Why Santiago is a useful case study

Chile sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire and is one of the most seismically active countries in the world, having experienced some of the largest earthquakes ever recorded, including in 1960 and 2010. As a result, structural design codes in Chile — including those governing elevated metro viaducts — place seismic performance above almost every other consideration. The Santiago Metro network is mostly underground, but some outlying sections run above ground on viaducts where terrain and lower building density make elevated construction more economical. That combination makes Santiago a useful reference point for how pier and viaduct design can be adapted to severe earthquake risk.

## Pier design

- **Foundations:** Piers are typically founded on bored piles or other deep foundations that penetrate weak surface layers to reach competent soil or rock. In seismic zones, pile count and diameter must also resist the large lateral and inertial forces generated during an earthquake, not just the vertical load from the structure above.
- **Pier shaft:** Following a "capacity design" philosophy, engineers deliberately designate a ductile zone — usually near the base or top of the pier — to absorb deformation during a major earthquake, while the foundation and the girder above are made stronger so they fail later, if at all. Dense transverse reinforcement in these ductile zones lets the pier deform without collapsing.
- **Seismic isolation bearings:** Many modern viaducts, including in Chile, use lead-rubber bearings or friction pendulum bearings between the top of the pier and the girder to decouple ground shaking from the structure above, substantially reducing the seismic force transmitted into the deck and stations.

## Viaduct girder design

- Girders are usually precast segmental units or prestressed concrete box girders, erected on the piers with a launching gantry.
- **Seismic restrainers/stoppers:** Fitted at both ends of each girder to prevent it from sliding off its bearings during large lateral displacements — a lesson learned the hard way from major earthquakes elsewhere in the world, where many bridge spans fell for lack of this detail.
- **Expansion joints:** Sized with enough movement capacity to absorb relative displacement between adjacent girder segments without impact damage or cracking.

## Overall seismic design philosophy

The general approach in highly seismic countries like Chile is to design for controlled, ductile deformation rather than pure rigidity, combined with structural redundancy so that the failure of one element doesn't cascade into a wider collapse. After a major earthquake, metro operators typically inspect every pier, bearing, and joint along the line before resuming train service.

## Lessons for other cities

Not every city faces Chile's level of seismic risk, but capacity design, seismic isolation bearings, and girder restrainers are increasingly standard practice for elevated metro lines in moderate-to-high seismic regions worldwide, serving as an added layer of passenger safety and helping systems recover quickly after a disaster.
