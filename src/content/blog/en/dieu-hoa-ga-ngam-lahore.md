## Why underground stations in hot climates need a dedicated cooling strategy

Lahore, like many large South Asian cities, has a long, intensely hot summer season. For metro lines with underground sections in such climates, air conditioning design is a fundamentally different problem than in temperate cities: the system must not only keep passengers comfortable but also absorb a very large background heat load flowing into the underground space from the hot ground and outside air, around the clock. If the system is undersized or poorly optimized, an underground station can end up more stifling than the street above during peak hours, directly hurting passenger comfort and safety.

## Heat sources an underground station must handle

An underground station in a hot climate faces several heat sources acting together:

- **Conducted heat from the surrounding ground and structure**, especially at shallow stations close to the surface, where ground temperature closely tracks the climate above.
- **Waste heat from trains**: regenerative braking, traction motors, and wheel-rail friction release heat continuously every time a train arrives or departs.
- **Heat given off by passengers**: at peak ridership, the combined body heat of a crowded platform is a significant load that preliminary estimates often underrate.
- **Heat from lighting, escalators, and other electrical equipment** running continuously inside the station.
- **Heat infiltration through entrances**, where hot outside air mixes directly with the station's interior every time passengers pass through.

## Architecture of a station air conditioning system

A station's air conditioning system is typically organized in three layers:

1. **Central chiller plant**: usually located at surface level or in a technical floor near the station, producing chilled water for the whole system. Placing the chiller where it's easy to access simplifies maintenance and heat rejection to the outside environment.
2. **Air Handling Units (AHUs)**: installed in a concourse-level or nearby plant room, these take chilled water from the plant to cool and filter air before distributing it to the station through ductwork.
3. **Air distribution at platform and concourse level**: cool air is delivered through supply diffusers along the station ceiling, while warm, used air is drawn back through return grilles, forming a closed circulation loop.

Unlike tunnel ventilation, which primarily serves fire safety and smoke extraction, a station's air conditioning system focuses on holding steady temperature and humidity in a crowded space — but the two systems must always be designed in coordination, with the ability to switch priority to emergency mode when needed.

## Energy-saving strategies in hot climates

Because the heat load is large and the system runs for many hours a day, electricity for air conditioning is a significant share of an underground station's operating cost in hot climates. Common strategies to reduce that load include:

- **Good thermal insulation of the station envelope** and limiting the area of entrances exposed directly to hot outside air, for example with automatic doors or air curtains at entry points.
- **Exploiting the day/night temperature difference**, running chillers at reduced capacity overnight when ambient temperatures drop.
- **Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs) for fans and pumps**, letting capacity track the actual heat load instead of always running at maximum.
- **Zoning**: cooling crowded areas like concourses and platforms more deeply, while low-traffic technical rooms rely on natural ventilation or minimal cooling.

## Operation and maintenance

A station's air conditioning system runs almost continuously, so routine maintenance matters as much as the original design: cooling coils and filters need regular cleaning to maintain heat-exchange efficiency, chilled-water piping needs periodic inspection to catch leaks early, and a building management system (BMS) should continuously monitor temperature and humidity across the station so capacity can be adjusted in step with actual passenger volume.

## Takeaways

Air conditioning design for underground stations in hot-climate cities like Lahore has to be sized on the system's real combined heat load — from the ground, from trains, and from passengers — starting at basic design stage, since chiller capacity and AHU space directly shape a station's technical floor plan. Experience from hot-climate cities shows that pairing architectural measures that limit heat infiltration with energy-efficient M&E solutions delivers better long-term operating performance than simply adding more chiller capacity.
