## Why underground structures respond to earthquakes differently

For a building, an earthquake generates inertial forces that make the structure sway, and engineers design it to resist that motion. For a tunnel or underground station, the situation is quite different: the structure is entirely surrounded by soil and rock, so it does not vibrate freely like a building — instead, it is forced to deform along with the surrounding ground as seismic waves pass through. In other words, the seismic challenge for underground structures lies less in inertial forces and more in the structure's ability to "keep up" with ground deformation without cracking or losing load-bearing capacity.

## Two key deformation modes to control

Seismic design for metro tunnels and stations generally focuses on two deformation mechanisms:

1. **Ovaling of circular tunnels**: when shear waves propagate perpendicular to the tunnel axis, the tunnel's circular cross-section cyclically distorts into an oval shape. The lining — whether precast segments or sprayed concrete — must be ductile enough to accommodate this distortion without brittle cracking at the joints between segments.
2. **Racking of rectangular station boxes**: underground stations built with diaphragm walls using cut-and-cover methods typically have a rectangular box cross-section. When the ground undergoes lateral shear displacement, the box is "racked" into a parallelogram shape. Columns and walls inside the station must be designed to withstand the relative displacement between roof slab and base slab without losing vertical load-carrying capacity.

## Flexible joints — a critical design detail

Another important principle is managing transition points between structural sections of differing stiffness — for example, where a circular tunnel connects to a box-shaped station, or where a tunnel passes from soft soil into hard rock. At these locations, the structure tends to move differently during an earthquake, which can concentrate stress if the connection is rigid. A common solution is to provide a flexible (seismic) joint — using resilient materials, a controlled expansion gap, or reinforcement detailing that allows limited relative rotation and sliding within safe bounds — so the two structural sections can move somewhat independently rather than transferring the full force directly into one another.

## Istanbul's experience

Istanbul lies close to the North Anatolian Fault, one of the most active and extensively studied fault zones in the world, so the city's major underground infrastructure projects — including the well-known Marmaray rail crossing beneath the Bosphorus Strait — have had to place seismic design high among planning priorities from the earliest stages. The general lesson is to assess seismic hazard according to the specific conditions of each alignment segment rather than applying one uniform load factor across an entire line, to prioritize materials and detailing capable of ductile deformation at sensitive locations, and to provide flexible joints at every significant structural or geological transition.

## Takeaways

Seismic design for underground structures calls for a different mindset than structures above ground: the goal is not to make the structure "stiffer" than the surrounding ground, but to make it deliberately flexible enough to deform together with the ground without failing. For cities in seismically active regions, integrating soil–structure interaction analysis and flexible joint detailing from the basic design stage onward is a decisive factor in the long-term safety of a metro line.
