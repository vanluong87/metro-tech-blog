## What platform screen doors are and why they matter

A Platform Screen Door (PSD) system is a set of glass panels and sliding doors installed along the edge of a station platform, fully separating the waiting area from the track. The doors open only once the train has stopped at the correct position and its own doors are opening at the same time, which almost entirely eliminates the risk of a passenger falling or stepping onto the tracks, whether accidentally or intentionally. This is why PSDs have become close to mandatory on new metro systems, especially on driverless, fully automated lines where there is no driver directly watching the platform before the doors close.

Beyond the safety function, PSDs bring real operational benefits: they keep hot air and dust from the tunnel out of the air-conditioned platform area, reducing the load on ventilation and cooling systems (particularly important in hot climates), cutting noise, and limiting objects falling onto the tracks that could disrupt train operations.

## Common types of platform screen doors

PSDs are generally classified by height and how fully they seal the platform:

1. **Full-Height PSD**: glass panels run from the platform floor to the station ceiling, forming a fully enclosed barrier between the waiting area and the track. This type offers the best thermal insulation, noise reduction, and safety performance, and is typically used in underground stations or stations with centralized air conditioning.
2. **Half-Height PSD**: panels and doors are only about 1.2–1.5 m tall, enough to stop a passenger from stepping or falling onto the tracks but without creating a fully sealed enclosure. This type is lighter and cheaper, and suits elevated or open-air stations where full climate separation isn't needed.

## Operating principle and signaling integration

The critical engineering challenge for PSDs isn't the sliding-door mechanism itself, but its precise synchronization with the train signaling system. The screen doors are only permitted to open once the automatic train control system (typically CBTC) confirms the train has stopped exactly at its designated stopping point on the platform, its speed is zero, and its own doors have received the open command. This entire interlock chain is designed on a fail-safe principle: if there's any stopping-position error or loss of signal, the PSDs simply do not open, prioritizing safety over throughput.

Each pair of doors on the PSD must align precisely with the door positions on every train type that runs on the line. This is an important design constraint to settle early, since if a line operates several train types with different door configurations, the PSD system must be designed with flexible door spacing, or the line must standardize on a single train type.

## Lessons from the Dubai Metro

The Dubai Metro is one of the largest fully automated, driverless (GoA4) metro systems in the world, and full-height PSDs have been installed at every station since opening. With no driver directly observing the platform, PSDs serve as an essential safety layer that replaces the platform-monitoring role a driver would normally play on more conventional systems. In addition, the region's hot, dusty desert climate makes the thermal and dust-isolation benefits of full-height PSDs especially valuable: the platform area stays reliably air-conditioned, fully separated from the hot air pushed along the tunnel by train movement (the piston effect).

## Takeaways

Platform screen doors shouldn't be treated as a late-stage finishing item bolted on at the end of a project. They need to be integrated into the signaling system design, station architecture, and rolling-stock selection from the basic design stage onward. For driverless, automated lines or lines operating in harsh climates, full-height PSDs are close to a hard requirement for both long-term operational safety and energy efficiency.
