## What is NATM?

The New Austrian Tunnelling Method (NATM) is a tunnelling philosophy that emerged in Austria in the mid-20th century, fundamentally different from earlier tunnelling approaches. Rather than treating the surrounding ground purely as a load to be fully supported by rigid structure, NATM treats that ground as part of the load-bearing system itself. The tunnel is excavated in short rounds, immediately stabilized with a flexible lining — typically shotcrete combined with rock bolts — and its deformation is then monitored so the construction approach can be adjusted before the permanent lining is completed.

## Core principles

NATM rests on three main principles:

1. **Mobilizing the ground's own self-supporting capacity**: as excavation proceeds, the surrounding rock or soil forms a natural load-bearing arch if it is reinforced promptly and appropriately, removing the need for the kind of absolute support used in traditional excavation.
2. **Flexible, staged excavation**: the tunnel cross-section is often divided into smaller parts — for example, the crown excavated first, followed by the bench and invert — to reduce the unsupported face area at any given moment, which is especially important in weak ground.
3. **Monitoring as an integral part of construction**: convergence, settlement, and lining-pressure instruments are installed continuously, allowing site engineers to adjust bolt density, shotcrete thickness, or excavation rate in real time rather than following a single fixed design for the entire alignment.

## Why Austria pioneered NATM

NATM grew out of the complex mountain geology of the Alps, where transport and hydropower tunnel projects often faced constantly changing ground conditions along a single alignment. A rigid, fixed-design-from-the-start approach proved inefficient and costly under these conditions, pushing Austrian engineers to develop a method that could adapt construction measures to the actual ground encountered rather than to assumptions made on the drawing board. This experience was later applied widely to metro lines in Vienna and other European cities, particularly in mixed-ground sections — weathered rock interspersed with soft soil — where a TBM struggles to adapt quickly.

## Advantages and applicable conditions

- **High flexibility with variable ground conditions**: well suited to tunnel sections where geology is heterogeneous and difficult to predict precisely from initial surveys.
- **Lower equipment investment than TBM**: no need for an expensive dedicated boring machine, making it suitable for short tunnel sections or irregular cross-sections such as underground stations or branch connections.
- **High demands on monitoring and field decision-making**: the technical team must be experienced enough to read monitoring data and adjust construction measures promptly, or the risk of localized collapse increases.

## Lessons worth applying elsewhere

Austria's experience shows that NATM's core value lies not in one fixed technical formula, but in an adaptive mindset: excavate, measure, and adjust as you go. For metro projects crossing complex geology or featuring large, irregular station cross-sections, combining NATM for these special sections with TBM for the main running tunnels is a strategy many cities around the world have adopted to optimize both cost and safety.
