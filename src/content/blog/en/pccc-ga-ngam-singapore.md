## Why underground stations need a different fire-safety mindset

An underground metro station packs a large number of passengers into an enclosed space, deep below ground, with only a limited number of fixed escape routes — usually stairs or escalators leading to the surface — and emergency vehicles cannot reach the scene directly the way they can at a high-rise building. Fire-safety design for an underground station is therefore not primarily about "fighting fire"; it is first and foremost about getting passengers out of the danger zone within the shortest possible time, before smoke reaches the path they are using to escape.

## Core components of station fire protection

- **Early fire detection**: smoke and heat sensors plus CCTV cameras densely installed throughout the concourse, platform, and technical rooms, all feeding into the operations control center (OCC) so decisions can be made within the first critical seconds.
- **Automatic fire suppression**: sprinkler heads in commercial and technical areas, paired with clean-agent gas suppression systems for rooms housing sensitive electrical and electronic equipment, such as signaling and power rooms.
- **Smoke extraction and stair pressurization**: ceiling-mounted smoke extraction fans combined with a pressurization system for escape stairwells, keeping stairwell pressure higher than the fire area so smoke cannot infiltrate the evacuation path.
- **Compartmentation**: fire-rated walls, doors, and partitions divide the station into separate compartments, slowing the spread of fire and smoke from one area to another long enough for passengers to get out.

## Designing escape routes: a problem of time and crowd flow

Unlike an ordinary building, escape-route sizing for an underground station is based on pedestrian flow modelling: the design assumes a fully loaded station at peak hour, plus the passengers from a train that has just made an emergency stop at the platform, with everyone required to reach the surface within a defined safe evacuation time — typically a matter of minutes. From that assumption, engineers determine the minimum number and width of escape stairs, treat escalators as a supplementary rather than a primary escape route, and identify choke points to avoid in the station layout. Temporary refuge areas may also be placed at transfer points between deep station levels, giving passengers a safe place to pause before continuing their ascent.

## Singapore's approach

Singapore's MRT system operates in a tropical climate and an extremely dense urban environment, so its underground stations are held to strict fire-safety standards, reviewed and approved by the Singapore Civil Defence Force (SCDF) from the earliest design stage. Large, multi-level stations typically include an independent emergency escape route leading directly to a street-level exit, separate from the normal entrances and exits, along with an emergency public-address system that gives passengers real-time instructions during an incident. Regular joint evacuation drills between the metro operator and emergency responders are also a mandatory part of ongoing operations, not just a one-time design requirement.

## Takeaways

Fire safety for an underground station cannot simply be the sum of individual fire-protection devices; it has to be engineered as one integrated system, where early detection, compartmentation, smoke control, and escape routes all work together, sized against realistic crowd-flow models rather than generic standards. The experience of dense networks like Singapore's shows that involving the fire authority in approvals from the basic-design stage, together with maintaining regular operational drills, delivers far more durable safety outcomes than simply meeting the minimum technical standard on paper.
