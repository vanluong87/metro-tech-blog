## Why noise and vibration are a distinct challenge for underground metros

Unlike most forms of environmental impact that can be measured and controlled at their source, metro-generated noise and vibration travel through the ground and the building structures themselves, reaching properties that have no direct connection to the project at all. Vibration from wheel-rail contact passes through the tunnel structure, into the surrounding soil or rock, and up into the foundations of buildings above — a phenomenon known as ground-borne vibration. In many cases it re-radiates as low-frequency, audible noise inside those buildings (ground-borne noise), even when the structure sits tens of meters from the track centerline. The issue is especially sensitive for buildings such as concert halls, recording studios, hospitals, or housing located close to the alignment.

## Where vibration comes from and how it travels

Vibration is generated mainly by wheel-rail interaction at discontinuities such as rail joints, switches, or small surface defects like wheel flats and rail corrugation. How much of that vibration reaches the buildings above depends on several factors: train speed, the type of track structure, the geotechnical properties of the ground (bedrock transmits vibration far more efficiently than clay or soft soil), tunnel depth, and the lateral distance from the track centerline to the building. Because ground-borne vibration is nearly impossible to block once it has left the tunnel and spread through the surrounding ground, the most effective control strategy is always to reduce vibration at the source or as close to the source as possible — before it has a chance to propagate.

## Engineering solutions for vibration control

A range of measures is applied depending on how sensitive the structures above a given section are:

- **Resilient rail fastening**: rubber or polyurethane pads placed between the rail and the sleeper, absorbing part of the vibration energy before it reaches the track bed.
- **Floating slab track**: the most effective but also the most expensive solution — the entire concrete slab carrying the rails "floats" on rubber bearings or springs, almost completely isolating it from the surrounding tunnel structure. Typically reserved for especially sensitive sections, such as beneath theaters or concert halls.
- **Periodic rail grinding**: removes microscopic surface defects on the rail, reducing vibration generated by wheel-rail interaction right at the source.
- **Speed restrictions in sensitive zones**: lowering train speed through sections near especially sensitive buildings, since vibration energy rises sharply with speed.

Deciding which solution to apply, and where along the alignment, has to be based on vibration surveys and predictions carried out during the design stage — it is not something that can be easily retrofitted once a line is already built.

## Stockholm's approach

Stockholm's metro (Tunnelbana) sits in a geologically favorable position for this kind of problem: most of the network runs through very hard, stable bedrock, unlike many other cities where tunnels must be driven through soft soil or clay. Bedrock allows large stretches of tunnel to be built without a full concrete lining along their entire length, but it is also an extremely efficient medium for transmitting vibration over long distances — so sections of the alignment running near sensitive buildings still require targeted damping measures at exactly those locations. The common approach in long-operating systems like Stockholm's is to combine detailed, section-by-section vibration surveys with targeted damping solutions, rather than applying one costly blanket solution across the entire line.

## Takeaways

Noise and vibration control is not a problem that can be solved with a single one-size-fits-all solution — the sensitivity of the buildings above, the geotechnical properties of the ground, and the operating speed at each section all determine what measure fits. Vibration surveys need to happen early, alongside tunnel structural design, since retrofitting damping measures after a line is already in operation is always far more expensive than integrating them from the start. The experience of networks running through bedrock, like Stockholm's, shows that favorable ground conditions for construction do not translate into relaxed vibration-control requirements — if anything, hard rock can demand even closer attention at sensitive points along the route.
