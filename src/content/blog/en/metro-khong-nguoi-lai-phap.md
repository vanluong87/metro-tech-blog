## What is driverless metro operation?

Driverless metro operation — also called unattended train operation (UTO) — is the highest grade of automation for urban rail, in which the train runs its entire journey on its own: starting, running, stopping at stations, and opening and closing doors, with no driver in the cab. The international classification (IEC 62290) defines four Grades of Automation (GoA), from GoA1 (a driver controls the train manually, with only automatic protection) up to GoA4 (fully unattended operation, with no staff on board even to handle abnormal situations). Most driverless metro lines today run at GoA4, built on a CBTC (Communications-Based Train Control) signaling foundation that uses moving-block separation to keep trains safely spaced.

## Technical requirements for unattended operation

For a metro line to move to GoA4, several technical conditions must be met at once:

1. **Platform screen doors**: these prevent passengers or objects from falling onto the tracks when no staff member is directly supervising the platform; they open only once the train has stopped at the correct position and in sync with the train's own doors.
2. **CBTC signaling with moving-block separation**: continuously and precisely tracks train position, calculates safe braking distance in real time, and adjusts speed automatically with no driver intervention.
3. **Intrusion detection along the running tunnel**: sensors or cameras detect foreign objects or people entering the track area and trigger an automatic emergency stop.
4. **An operations control center (OCC) with centralized video monitoring**: controllers watch the whole line through cameras and system data and can intervene remotely — stopping a train, opening doors in an emergency, or switching to local manual control — when needed.
5. **Procedures for abnormal situations**: clear protocols for fire, a passenger medical emergency, or a train stopped mid-tunnel, including dispatching a staff member to the scene when necessary.

## How operation and maintenance differ from a traditional metro

On a driverless line, the human role doesn't disappear — it shifts, from driving in the cab to monitoring the system from a control center and responding to incidents on site. Maintenance also has to meet a noticeably higher reliability bar, since there is no longer a driver to notice and report anomalies during daily operation; every sensor, signaling component, and platform screen door must be inspected and preventively maintained on a strict schedule, because a small fault in these systems can directly affect operational safety. Driverless trains also typically have a different interior layout with no driver's cab, often with a forward-facing view at the front and rear cars instead, and maintenance and operations staff need retraining to work with automated control software and remote-monitoring procedures rather than traditional driving skills.

## The conversion experience on Paris Métro Line 1

Métro Line 1 is one of the oldest and busiest underground lines in Paris. It was converted from staffed operation to fully automated (GoA4) running in the early 2010s, becoming one of the best-known examples of upgrading an already operating metro line to the highest grade of automation without building an entirely new line. The conversion required installing platform screen doors at every station on the line, replacing the older signaling system with CBTC, introducing new automation-compatible rolling stock, and keeping the line in commercial service throughout the upgrade — a genuinely complex undertaking, since construction, testing, and the switchover had to proceed section by section without shutting the whole line down. The Paris experience shows that converting an existing line to driverless operation is generally more difficult than designing a fully automated line from scratch, since the conversion must work within infrastructure and station layouts that already exist.

## Takeaways

Driverless metro operation brings clear benefits: higher achievable train frequency, more flexibility to adjust timetables to real demand, and lower long-term driver staffing costs. Those benefits come with a significant investment in safety infrastructure — platform screen doors, CBTC signaling, centralized monitoring — and a far more tightly organized system of preventive maintenance and remote surveillance than a traditional metro requires. Conversion projects on existing lines, such as Paris Métro Line 1, show that this is a complex engineering and operational undertaking that needs careful planning from the earliest stages of an infrastructure upgrade program.
