## What TOD means, and why it has to start early

Transit-oriented development (TOD) is an urban planning approach where building density and mixed land uses — housing, offices, retail, public services — are concentrated most heavily within walking distance of a station, then taper off further out. What sets TOD apart from simply "building a station and letting the market develop around it" is that land-use planning and metro line planning are designed together, starting at the pre-feasibility stage: station locations are chosen partly for their development potential, and in turn the surrounding land-use plan is shaped to maximize walking and cycling access to the station. Copenhagen is one of the most widely studied examples of this approach, with the Copenhagen Metro planned as an integral part of the city's long-term urban development strategy.

## The Finger Plan and corridor-based growth

Since the mid-20th century, Denmark has followed a regional planning strategy for Copenhagen known as the "Finger Plan" — urban growth radiating outward from the center along public transport corridors, like fingers, with green wedges preserved between them to prevent unchecked sprawl. As the Copenhagen Metro and later its circle line extensions were added, this principle was carried down to a finer scale: each new station, especially in newly developed areas such as Ørestad, came paired with a high-density, mixed-use land plan for its immediate surroundings, while areas further from the station kept lower density or were set aside as public open space.

## Planning principles behind the integration

- **Station siting driven by development potential**: station locations are chosen not only for technical reasons (ground conditions, construction cost) but also for how much of the surrounding land can realistically be redeveloped at higher density.
- **Density and use regulated by distance from the station**: local area plans typically set a floor area ratio (FAR) that decreases with distance from the station, favoring residential and office uses nearby and lower-trip-generating uses further out.
- **Priority given to pedestrians and cyclists around stations**: station squares, covered walkways, and large-scale bicycle parking are designed alongside the station itself to reduce reliance on private cars for first/last-mile trips.
- **Infrastructure timed to match land development**: the metro line and local utilities — power, water, wastewater — are scheduled in step with the sale or lease of development plots in new growth areas, avoiding a finished station sitting next to vacant land for years.

## Land value capture as a financing mechanism

A distinctive feature of Copenhagen's model is using the rise in land value created by metro access to help fund the infrastructure itself. In Ørestad, publicly held land was replanned as a new high-density district along the metro line, and part of the proceeds from selling or leasing development rights to that land went toward financing the metro project. This approach eases the burden on public transport budgets while also giving planning authorities and the metro operator a shared financial stake in coordinating land-use and network planning closely from the outset.

## Lessons for cities building out their metro networks

Copenhagen's experience shows that TOD delivers the most value when it is built into planning from the moment alignments and station locations are chosen, not added after a line is already built. For cities early in developing their metro networks, setting up a formal coordination mechanism between urban planning authorities and the metro project owner, backed by legal tools that allow land-use plans to be adjusted alongside line planning, is an important condition for turning each new station into a thriving urban center rather than just a transfer point.
