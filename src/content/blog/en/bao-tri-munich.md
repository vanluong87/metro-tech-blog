## From corrective to preventive maintenance

Once a metro line enters commercial service, the engineering focus shifts from construction to keeping the system running safely and on time over a design life of several decades. The most old-fashioned approach — fixing equipment only after it fails ("corrective maintenance") — carries serious risk for a system carrying hundreds of thousands of passengers a day, since an unexpected failure during peak hours can cause major disruption. For this reason, modern metro operators rely on preventive maintenance: inspecting, servicing, and replacing components on a planned schedule before they degrade to the point of failure, based on expected service life, operating hours, or distance run.

## Core preventive maintenance strategies

In practice, operators typically combine several strategies:

1. **Time-based maintenance**: components are inspected or replaced on a fixed time or distance cycle, regardless of actual condition — simple to plan, but can replace parts that are still in good condition earlier than strictly necessary.
2. **Condition-based maintenance**: sensors and measuring equipment on trains, track, and signaling systems continuously collect data (vibration, temperature, wear, current draw, and more); maintenance is triggered only when the data shows signs of degradation, which optimizes cost and reduces unnecessary downtime.
3. **Predictive maintenance**: building on condition-based maintenance, this approach uses historical data and trend analysis to forecast when a component is likely to fail, allowing intervention to be scheduled before a failure actually occurs.
4. **Scheduled visual and instrumented inspection**: track inspection cars, ultrasonic testing of rail welds, and visual inspection of tunnel and station structures on a fixed schedule, complementing automated sensor data.

## The night maintenance window — the scarcest resource

For metro lines that run all day and stop trains for only a few hours at night, the "maintenance window" — the period with no commercial trains on the line — is an especially scarce resource. All track, signaling, power, and structural maintenance work must be carefully planned to fit within this window, often only about three to four hours a night, before commercial service resumes early the next morning. Coordinating multiple maintenance crews working simultaneously on different tasks, ensuring worker safety in a confined space, and closing and reopening the track on schedule is a logistics challenge every bit as complex as the original construction work.

## The approach on Munich's U-Bahn

The U-Bahn (underground railway) system in Munich, Germany, operates within a German engineering tradition well known for systematic processes and strict adherence to procedure in maintaining public infrastructure. Like many European urban rail systems, Munich's U-Bahn follows a cyclical preventive maintenance plan covering rolling stock, track, signaling, and power supply, combined with scheduled instrumented inspection. Core maintenance work is carried out during the nightly non-operating window, when commercial service pauses for a few hours. German engineering culture's emphasis on documented procedures and periodic certification against technical standards also shapes the systematic way maintenance is organized across major German cities more broadly.

## Takeaways

Preventive maintenance is not a single engineering task but an ongoing operational management system, requiring investment in sensors, asset management software, a skilled technical workforce, and — just as importantly — rigorous scheduling to make the most of a narrow daily maintenance window. Metro systems with long track records of safe, reliable operation tend to share one trait: they treat preventive maintenance as an inseparable part of operations, resourced appropriately from the project planning stage onward, rather than as an afterthought once the system opens.
