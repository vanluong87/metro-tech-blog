## The problem: evacuating from the middle of a long tunnel

Unlike an underground station, which always has stairs or escalators leading straight to the surface, a train that stalls in the middle of a tunnel section several kilometers long may be hundreds of meters to several kilometers from the nearest station, with no immediate way out. If a fire breaks out, traction power fails for an extended period, or water ingress occurs, passengers have no choice but to walk along the tunnel under limited lighting and air quality. For this reason, modern metro lines — especially those built with twin bored tunnels using TBMs — must include an active escape strategy built into the tunnel itself, not one that relies solely on stations.

## What a cross-passage is

The most common solution for a twin-bore configuration is to build cross-passages: short connecting tunnels that link the two parallel running tunnels directly. The principle is simple: if an incident — fire, smoke, a stalled train — occurs in one tunnel, passengers cross through the cross-passage into the opposite bore, which remains smoke-free, and continue toward the nearest station or wait there for a rescue train approaching from the safe direction. Cross-passages are typically spaced 200-300 meters apart along the alignment, fitted with fire-rated doors, emergency lighting, and photoluminescent signage so passengers can orient themselves even in smoke or during a power outage.

## Supporting elements of tunnel escape design

- **Walkway**: a raised walking path running continuously along the full tunnel length, wide enough for a single file of people, kept clear of the track bed and traction power equipment.
- **Emergency lighting and signage**: emergency lights on a dedicated backup power supply, together with distance-to-exit markers placed at regular intervals so passengers always know which direction leads to the nearest escape point.
- **Emergency ventilation**: tunnel jet fans or vertical ventilation shafts at technical shafts, capable of reversing direction to push smoke away from the direction passengers are moving.
- **Emergency communication**: emergency telephones or call points placed along the walkway, connected directly to the operations control center to report location and status.

## Cairo Metro's approach

Cairo Metro is one of the oldest and largest metro systems in the Middle East and North Africa region, running long twin-bore tunnel sections through the densely built-up areas along the Nile. Given Cairo's geology and urban layout, many tunnel sections sit far from the nearest station for extended stretches, so newer TBM-built lines treat regularly spaced cross-passages as a mandatory part of the tunnel structural design from the outset, rather than adding them later. Cross-passage spacing is calculated using the same time-based evacuation modelling applied on other long-tunnel metro systems worldwide, while also accounting for Cairo's hot climate and the correspondingly greater reliance on mechanical ventilation compared with temperate-climate systems. Coordination between the operator and civil emergency services on tunnel evacuation drills is also treated as an ongoing part of safe operation, not a one-time design requirement satisfied at construction.

## Takeaways

Escape design for long tunnels cannot rely solely on distance to the nearest station; the tunnel itself must be treated as a space that needs its own internal means of escape, with cross-passages, walkways, emergency lighting, and ventilation working together. The experience of long-tunnel networks like Cairo's shows that building cross-passage spacing and emergency ventilation strategy into the initial structural design — rather than treating them as an add-on — meaningfully reduces both cost and operational risk later on.
