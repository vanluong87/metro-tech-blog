## Why automation matters more and more in tunneling

The Kyiv Metro is known for having some of the deepest stations of any metro system in the world, a result of the alignment having to cross hilly terrain along the Dnieper River through ground with a high water table and soft soils interbedded with looser layers. Building tunnels under such complex geology and at such depth demands a high degree of precision and worker safety, since even small errors during excavation or lining installation can lead to alignment deviation, water ingress, or face instability. This is exactly why automation and robotics are increasingly applied in modern metro tunneling: machines can repeat operations with a precision beyond what a human operator can sustain, while also reducing the number of workers who need to be physically present at the tunnel face — the highest-risk zone on any underground site.

## Key areas of automation

- **Automated guidance for tunnel boring machines (TBMs)**: laser systems, gyroscopes, and continuous positioning sensors compare the cutter head's actual position against the design alignment in real time, automatically adjusting the thrust angle of the hydraulic jacks to keep the TBM on line within a few millimeters of tolerance.
- **Robotic segment erectors**: a robotic arm lifts and positions each precast concrete lining segment into its exact place in the ring, replacing manual handling of components weighing several tons in a confined space.
- **Automated material transport**: rail-guided wagons or conveyor systems automatically move excavated spoil and construction materials along the tunnel, cutting down on manned trips through an enclosed environment.
- **Inspection robots and drones**: rail-mounted robots or small aerial units fitted with cameras and 3D laser scanning (LiDAR) examine the tunnel lining surface, catching early signs of cracking or water infiltration without needing scaffolding or sending workers into unfinished sections.

## Benefits: safety, productivity, and consistent quality

The clearest benefit of automation is reducing how often workers must be directly exposed to the most hazardous parts of an underground site — an active tunnel face, a heavy segment-erection zone, or sections whose stability has not yet been fully verified. Automated guidance and erection systems also maintain consistent precision along the full length of an alignment, unaffected by fatigue or lapses in judgment that can creep in after long shifts. Data collected by TBM sensors and inspection robots additionally builds up a detailed digital record of the construction process, which proves valuable later for maintenance planning and structural assessment.

## Challenges in difficult ground conditions

Despite its advantages, deploying automation on projects with complex geology, like those in Kyiv, comes with its own challenges. Ground that alternates between layers of very different character means TBM sensor systems need frequent recalibration to reflect actual conditions, otherwise the machine risks steering automatically based on data that no longer matches reality. The upfront investment in automated equipment and specialized robots also tends to be higher than for conventional methods, requiring a well-trained operating team capable of monitoring, recalibrating, and intervening whenever the automated system encounters a fault or an abnormal sensor reading.

## Where the trend is heading

The broader direction in metro tunneling is toward semi-automated and eventually fully automated systems, where the human role gradually shifts from direct manual operation to supervision and decision-making based on real-time data. For projects built under difficult ground conditions and demanding safety requirements, like those in Kyiv, combining automated guidance, robotic erection, and inspection robots does more than speed up progress — it is a practical way to reduce risk for workers in one of the most demanding working environments in the construction industry.
