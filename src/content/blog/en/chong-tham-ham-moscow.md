## Why waterproofing is a life-or-death requirement for metro tunnels

Most metro tunnels sit below the groundwater table, so their linings face constant water pressure for the entire design life of the structure — typically 100 years or more. A single unsealed crack or poorly sealed joint can lead to long-term seepage, corroding reinforcement, washing out fines from the surrounding ground, and damaging electrical and signaling equipment housed inside the tunnel. Waterproofing is therefore not a secondary finishing layer; it is an engineered system designed together with the tunnel structure from day one.

## The layered waterproofing system in a segmental lining

A typical segmental tunnel lining (installed behind a TBM) relies on several independent lines of defense against water:

1. **The concrete segment itself**: high-grade, low-permeability concrete that resists water migration through the material itself.
2. **Gaskets at the joints between segments**: usually EPDM rubber, often hydrophilic — swelling on contact with water to self-seal small gaps caused by installation tolerances or long-term structural movement.
3. **An external waterproofing membrane** (for tunnels built with other methods such as NATM or cut-and-cover): a PVC or bentonite membrane placed between the temporary support and the permanent concrete lining, acting as a full "raincoat" around the structure.
4. **A backup drainage system**: channels running along the tunnel invert that collect any residual seepage and direct it to sumps and pump stations, handling the small amount of water that inevitably gets through the primary barriers over time.

## Lessons from the Moscow Metro

The Moscow Metro is one of the deepest major metro systems in the world, with many stations built tens of meters underground to get below weak soil layers and groundwater pockets, and to avoid disturbing the city's dense surface development. Tunneling through strata that include clay, water-bearing sand, and karstic limestone has required:

- **Detailed hydrogeological survey** before setting the alignment's depth and choosing a construction method, to avoid high-pressure water pockets or karst cavities that could cause instability.
- **Durable, multi-layer linings that evolve over time**: older lines historically used cast-iron segmental linings valued for mechanical strength, while newer lines have moved toward reinforced concrete combined with swelling gaskets, which is easier to inspect and maintain.
- **Routine monitoring for moisture and leaks** throughout operation, catching early signs of deterioration so that localized repairs — such as grouting — can be made before damage spreads.

## Drainage systems and long-term maintenance

Even with the best waterproofing design, some small amount of water seepage over decades of operation is essentially unavoidable. That is why every modern metro system includes a continuous network of drainage channels, collection sumps, and automatic pump stations along the alignment, ensuring water is carried away before it can accumulate and affect the track, electrical systems, or tunnel invert.

## Lessons for other cities

For cities with a high groundwater table, such as Ho Chi Minh City, waterproofing design cannot rely on a single material layer alone. The Moscow Metro's experience illustrates a "defense in depth" approach — combining high-quality concrete, joint gaskets, external membranes, and backup drainage — as the most durable strategy for keeping underground infrastructure stable for decades of service.
