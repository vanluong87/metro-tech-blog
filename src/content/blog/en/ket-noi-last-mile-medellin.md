## Why the "last mile" matters

A metro line only delivers its full value when passengers can actually reach a station conveniently and get from the station to where they're going. The distance between home or work and the nearest station — commonly called the "last mile" — largely determines whether people choose to ride the metro at all, no matter how modern the main line is. This challenge is especially acute in cities with difficult terrain or dense neighborhoods set back from the main transit corridor. Medellín, Colombia's second-largest city, is one of the most widely studied examples of how to solve this problem, thanks to its steep hillside terrain and informally developed neighborhoods (*barrios*) surrounding the central valley where the Medellín Metro runs.

## Three layers of connectivity around the main line

Rather than relying solely on the urban rail line running along the valley floor, Medellín's public transport system was built as a multi-layered network of complementary modes:

- **Feeder buses**: small and medium buses run circulator routes through residential areas, carrying passengers from narrow streets and alleys unsuitable for larger vehicles to metro stations or interchange hubs, timed to connect with train schedules.
- **Urban cable car (Metrocable)**: this is the most distinctive feature of Medellín's model — gondola-style aerial cable car lines built as a formal part of the public transit system, connecting directly from valley-floor metro stations up to hillside neighborhoods that conventional buses struggle to serve efficiently. Passengers use the same fare and ticketing system as the metro, treating the cable car as another "line" within the network rather than a separate mode.
- **Walking and cycling infrastructure**: areas around stations are designed with covered walkways, pedestrian overpasses, bike parking, and public bike-share docking stations, allowing passengers within a reasonable walking distance (typically a 400-800 meter radius) to reach the station without needing an intermediate mode.

## Integration design principles

Medellín's experience highlights several principles with broad applicability:

1. **A single integrated fare system**: passengers tap once for an entire journey combining metro, cable car, and bus, avoiding the need to purchase separate tickets for each mode — often the single biggest barrier discouraging people from switching between modes.
2. **Interchange stations designed as one point, not scattered stops**: bus bays, cable car terminals, and metro fare gates are placed as close together as possible, minimizing walking time and distance between modes.
3. **Matching the last-mile mode to terrain and density**: where terrain is too steep for buses, a cable car is often more cost-effective over the long run than building new roads; where terrain is flat, feeder buses remain the more flexible and cheaper option.
4. **Accompanying social impact**: connecting low-income hillside neighborhoods to the city center via cable car does more than solve a transport problem — it also cuts travel times and improves access to jobs and public services for residents on the urban periphery.

## Lessons for other cities

Not every city has terrain suited to a cable car, but Medellín's core principle — treating the last mile as a formal part of metro system design, rather than something passengers are left to solve on their own — can be applied anywhere. For cities expanding their metro networks where many neighborhoods fall outside a comfortable walking radius, planning bus bays, cycling infrastructure, and safe pedestrian routes together from the earliest station design stage, paired with a unified fare system, typically yields a far greater boost in ridership than simply extending the main line further.
