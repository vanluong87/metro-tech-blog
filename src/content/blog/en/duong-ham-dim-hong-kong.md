## What is an immersed tube tunnel?

An immersed tube tunnel is a method for crossing a river or strait by prefabricating tunnel elements — large concrete or steel tube sections — at a dedicated casting site, floating them out to the alignment, lowering them into a trench dredged along the riverbed or seabed, and joining them end to end to form a continuous tunnel. It's a common choice for shallow-to-moderate-depth water crossings, where deep boring under the seabed with a TBM or NATM would be far more costly and risky.

## The basic construction sequence

Building an immersed tube tunnel generally follows these steps:

1. **Casting the tunnel elements**: each element (commonly 100-180m long, though lengths vary by project) is cast in a dry dock or a floating casting basin near the alignment, with both ends temporarily sealed by bulkheads so the element can float.
2. **Dredging the foundation trench**: a trench is dredged along the riverbed or seabed to the designed elevation and grade, typically topped with a bedding layer of loose material such as sand or gravel to provide a stable base for the element.
3. **Towing and sinking**: each element is towed to its installation position by tugboats, then ballast tanks are flooded to gradually sink it into place within the trench, under tight control from positioning and monitoring systems.
4. **Joining the elements**: adjoining elements are pressed together using differential water pressure acting on a gasket at the joint, after which the connection is completed with concrete structure and a permanent waterproofing layer.
5. **Protective backfill and seabed reinstatement**: once installed, each element is covered with loose fill to protect the structure from ship anchors, currents, and scour.

## Why Hong Kong has built up so much experience

Hong Kong's terrain is shaped by numerous straits and deep-water harbours that divide its urban areas, so both road and rail routes repeatedly need to cross water at multiple points across the network. Because the seabed in many of these areas is relatively shallow and dredgeable, the immersed tube tunnel has become a solution applied repeatedly for both road tunnels and rail crossings linking islands and peninsulas. Experience accumulated across multiple projects has helped Hong Kong build strong capability in offshore construction management — from large-scale element casting to precise positioning control when sinking elements amid the complex tides and currents of a working harbour.

## Key technical challenges

- **Positioning accuracy during sinking**: tolerances are very tight, requiring differential GPS, depth-monitoring instruments, and an experienced operations team to ensure each new element aligns precisely with the one already installed.
- **Waterproofing at the joints**: the connection between elements is the most sensitive point for water ingress; gasket design and the joining procedure must be rigorously tested before the tunnel enters service.
- **Impact on shipping traffic and the seabed environment**: dredging and the marine transport of tunnel elements require close coordination with port authorities to avoid disrupting shipping lanes or harming the seabed ecosystem.

## Lessons worth applying elsewhere

Hong Kong's experience shows that immersed tube tunnels are best suited to shallow water crossings, where the cost and risk of deep boring would outweigh the benefit. The method's success depends far more on offshore construction management capability — casting, transport, sinking, and joining — than on any single piece of technology. For cities planning metro lines that must cross a river or bay, carefully assessing seabed conditions and marine construction capability as early as the planning stage is the key factor in choosing an immersed tube tunnel over traditional deep-tunnelling methods.
