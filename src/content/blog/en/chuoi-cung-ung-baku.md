## Why the supply chain is a major risk in metro projects

A metro line consumes an enormous and diverse volume of materials: hundreds of thousands of cubic meters of concrete, tens of thousands of tons of rebar, thousands of precast tunnel lining segments, steel rails, signaling systems, rolling stock, and imported M&E equipment. Unlike ordinary civil works, many of these items simply aren't available on the domestic market, especially in countries building their first metro network or expanding one on a large scale, as is the case in Azerbaijan. In that situation, the supply chain stops being a routine procurement matter and becomes a critical schedule risk: a batch of tunnel lining segments arriving even a few weeks late can halt an active tunnel boring machine (TBM), triggering very costly downtime. At Baku Metro, where expansion lines must coordinate both domestic materials and imports from multiple countries, supply chain planning is treated as an inseparable part of the overall project plan, running in lockstep with construction progress.

## Classifying materials by supply risk

- **Materials produced on site or domestically**: concrete batched at a site plant, rebar fabricated to design — lower risk since output can be adjusted flexibly against schedule, though input quality (cement, aggregate, steel billet) still needs control.
- **Specialized materials produced regionally**: precast tunnel lining segments are typically cast at a temporary plant near the site or in the surrounding region, requiring formwork, curing, and transport to be planned in sync with the TBM's advance rate.
- **Imported equipment and materials**: TBM cutterheads, signaling systems, rolling stock, and specialized ventilation or fire-safety equipment usually must be imported from international manufacturers — this category has the longest lead times, sometimes over a year, and is directly exposed to customs procedures, international shipping, and geopolitical disruption.

## The role of transit warehouses and safety stock

To reduce the risk of disruption, metro projects commonly set up one or more transit warehouses near the site, acting as a buffer between suppliers and the real pace of construction. Critical materials — particularly imported components with long lead times — are ordered early and held at a "safety stock" level sufficient to absorb minor delivery fluctuations without slowing the site down. For bulky items like tunnel lining segments, storage yards need to be planned from the site layout design stage onward, sized according to the TBM's expected advance rate and the curing time segments need before they reach sufficient strength for installation.

## Coordinating procurement, customs, and international logistics

For imported materials, a metro project's supply chain often spans multiple countries: from the manufacturing plant, through a seaport or border crossing, to the site. For a country like Azerbaijan, positioned along Eurasian transport corridors, the choice of shipping route (sea, rail, or multimodal combinations) directly affects delivery time and reliability. The project's supply chain team needs to work closely with customs specialists to prepare import documentation well in advance, avoiding situations where equipment is held at port for lack of permits or paperwork — a common cause of delay on large infrastructure projects that depend heavily on imports.

## Tracking supply progress and early warning

Modern metro projects typically use order-tracking systems integrated with the overall construction schedule (often linked to a Gantt schedule or a 4D BIM model), letting the project team spot the risk of a specific shipment running late early enough to act before it threatens the critical path. Each key supplier usually has a detailed supply plan with checkpoint milestones — production completion, packaging, shipment departure, port arrival, site arrival — making it easier to catch deviations from plan early. For metro projects in countries still building out their networks, such as Baku, developing professional supply chain management capability — including supplier diversification, sensible safety stock levels, and early-warning tracking — is just as important as the engineering work itself in keeping the overall project on schedule.
