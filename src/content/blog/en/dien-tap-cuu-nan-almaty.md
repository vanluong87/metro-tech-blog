## Why emergency response needs a training track of its own

Ordinary operations training — driving a train by the book, monitoring on schedule, maintaining equipment on cycle — only prepares staff for the situations they face every day. A genuine emergency — an on-board fire, a train stuck mid-tunnel, a system-wide power loss, an injured passenger — is a different kind of problem: rare, fast-moving, and one where mistakes in the first few minutes can make things worse very quickly. For that reason, most metro systems worldwide run a dedicated emergency response training track alongside regular operations and maintenance training, aimed specifically at building the reflex to make the right call under time pressure and incomplete information.

## The typical structure of an emergency response program

1. **Classroom training on incident types and standard operating procedures (SOPs)**: staff learn the response procedure for each incident type — fire, smoke, a train stuck in a tunnel, a signaling failure, an injured passenger — along with the mandatory reporting and communication chain with the control center.
2. **Simulator-based training**: operators and controllers practice decision-making on simulators that replicate rare emergency scenarios, repeated enough times to build the right reflexes without any real-world risk.
3. **Passenger evacuation drills**: practicing the procedure for getting passengers off a train and moving them along tunnel or station escape routes in a controlled way that avoids panic.
4. **Multi-agency coordination drills**: large-scale exercises involving external fire and rescue services and emergency medical teams, not just metro operations staff, usually held periodically (annually or as safety regulations require) on the live system, with or without a mock train and simulated passengers.
5. **Post-drill debriefs**: after every drill or real incident, the parties involved review response times, communication bottlenecks, and adjust procedures as needed.

## The role of multi-agency coordination drills

A key difference between emergency response training and ordinary operations training is the involvement of agencies outside the metro operator itself — particularly fire services and emergency medical teams. In a real incident, how quickly outside rescuers can reach a train in distress inside a tunnel depends heavily on whether they are already familiar with the tunnel layout, the access points, and the communication procedure with the metro control center. For this reason, multi-agency coordination drills — even though they take time to organize and sometimes require pausing part of the system's operations — are treated as mandatory at many metro systems, not merely a nice-to-have activity.

## Almaty Metro's approach

Almaty Metro is Kazakhstan's only subway system, still modest in scale compared with many longer-established metro networks, with a limited number of stations and trains. This small scale creates a particular dynamic for emergency response training: fewer staff need to be trained, but each person typically has to take on more roles during an emergency than would be the case at larger systems with dedicated specialists for every function. As a result, training programs at smaller-scale systems tend to emphasize multi-skilling, so that a single operations staff member can take part in several steps of the response procedure at once. Regular coordination drills with the city's fire and rescue services are also an essential part of this, since the operator cannot handle the full rescue effort alone and depends on the city's broader emergency response capacity.

## Takeaways

Emergency response training cannot stop at handing staff a written procedure to read, because the reflex to act correctly under pressure only forms through repeated practice — on simulators and on the live system. Experience from systems of very different scale, from Almaty to much larger networks, shows that regular drills involving outside rescue services, combined with a serious debrief after every drill, are what actually determine how ready a metro system is when a real emergency happens — regardless of whether the system itself is large or small.
