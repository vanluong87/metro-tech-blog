## Why underground safety is a distinct challenge

Tunnel and underground station construction differs fundamentally from surface building work: crews operate in enclosed spaces, with limited escape routes, little natural airflow, and often below the water table. The hazards involved are specific to this environment: oxygen deficiency or toxic gas buildup (carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, methane from the ground), collapse of trench or shaft walls, falling objects in vertical shafts, sudden flooding, and fire in confined spaces that are hard to evacuate. Taipei, whose metro network (Taipei MRT) has been built and expanded continuously over several decades, offers a useful example of how a city gradually matures its occupational safety management system for this complex type of construction.

## Common safety management principles

- **Permit-to-work systems**: before entering a confined space — a vertical shaft, an enclosed tunnel section, a machine chamber — workers must hold a permit issued only after air quality checks, with a dedicated outside watcher and a clear evacuation plan in place.
- **Continuous gas monitoring**: fixed or personal gas detectors track oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and flammable gas levels, triggering automatic alarms when thresholds are exceeded, paired with forced ventilation to keep the tunnel atmosphere breathable.
- **Temporary support control**: shoring, diaphragm walls, and bracing inside excavated shafts must be designed, built, and inspected on a regular schedule to prevent collapse, especially where local geology includes soft soil layers or a high water table.
- **Full personal protective equipment (PPE)**: hard hats, puncture-resistant boots, high-visibility clothing, and self-rescue breathing devices carried at all times when working deep underground.
- **Emergency response planning**: temporary refuge stations, escape routes kept independent from material haulage routes, and emergency response teams that drill regularly.

## Management experience from Taipei

Over the course of building and expanding the Taipei MRT, the city's urban transit authority progressively developed strict occupational safety requirements applied to every underground construction contractor, including mandatory safety training before site access, periodic safety inspections independent of the contractor, and transparent incident reporting so lessons carry over into later contracts. This approach reflects a broader trend across many Asian cities: treating occupational safety as a contractor qualification criterion starting at the tender stage, rather than only a compliance requirement during construction.

## The role of safety culture

Beyond procedures and equipment, many metro projects have found that the safety culture of the whole team — from project managers down to frontline workers — is what ultimately determines long-term outcomes. This shows up in practices such as encouraging workers to proactively report unsafe conditions without fear of reprimand, holding short toolbox talks at the start of every shift, and tying safety responsibility into the performance evaluation of site management generally, not just the dedicated safety department.

## General lessons

Experience from underground construction sites shows that effective occupational safety requires three elements working together: engineering controls (gas monitoring, temporary support, ventilation), management processes (permit-to-work, independent inspection), and human culture (proactive reporting, shared responsibility). Missing any one of the three, accident risk in underground spaces — already considerably higher than above-ground construction — is difficult to bring fully under control, regardless of a project's scale or how modern its technology is.
