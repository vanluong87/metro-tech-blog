## Why interface management is a major challenge

A modern metro line is rarely built by a single contractor from start to finish. Instead, projects are typically split into several separate contract packages — civil structures (tunnels, stations), track and traction power, signalling and communications, and station mechanical/electrical fit-out — each delivered by a different contractor or joint venture, on its own schedule and contract terms. As the Metroul Bucureşti network has expanded with new lines, this parallel multi-package approach has let each contractor focus on its area of expertise and shortened the overall construction timeline. But that same division creates the project's biggest risk: the "interfaces" where one contract's scope ends and another's begins. If two contractors fail to agree on dimensions, tolerances, or timing at these points, the result is schedule delay, cost overruns, or in the worst case, rework that must be demolished and rebuilt.

## The main types of construction interfaces

- **Physical interfaces**: dimensions and locations where two packages' work must match exactly — for example, a cable duct or sleeve cast into the tunnel lining by the civil contractor must be the correct size for the systems contractor to later install track and cabling through it.
- **Design interfaces**: shared technical parameters between packages, such as a station floor's design load capacity matching the equipment the M&E package will install, or tunnel clearance being sufficient for the rolling stock package's trains.
- **Schedule interfaces**: the sequence of site handovers — the civil package must complete and formally hand over a tunnel section before the track package is allowed to begin work there, creating a tight chain of dependencies between contracts.
- **Information interfaces**: technical data (drawings, BIM models, inspection records) that must be shared in the right format and at the right time between contractors so each can complete the detailed design of its own scope.

## The interface register and control process

The central tool for managing this risk is the interface register — a living list of every identified interface point, the two (or more) packages involved, the information to be exchanged, the responsible person on each side, and its current status (under negotiation, agreed, constructed, verified). Each significant interface is formalized through an interface control document or agreement, signed by both contractors confirming the agreed parameters. Whenever a design change affects an already-agreed interface, an interface change control process requires that both parties be notified and agree before the change is implemented — preventing one contractor from altering something the other side does not know about.

## The role of interface coordination

Because individual contractors are naturally focused on protecting their own contractual interests, the project owner or an independent project management/delivery partner typically sets up a dedicated interface coordination function that sits between the packages. This team runs regular interface meetings with technical representatives from each contract to review the register, resolve open items, and update statuses. This arbitration role is especially important when two packages have conflicting incentives — for instance, a civil contractor wanting looser construction tolerances to save cost, while the systems contractor needs tighter tolerances to ensure accurate equipment installation.

## Lessons for multi-contract metro projects

Experience from Bucharest's metro network expansion shows that interface management must be established as early as the tender documentation stage, not left until construction is underway. Each package's contract should clearly define responsibilities for providing and receiving interface information, along with a dispute-resolution mechanism for when two contractors cannot agree. Investing in a transparent, regularly updated interface register, closely overseen by the project owner, is often the deciding factor in avoiding the most costly bottlenecks on metro projects split across multiple contracts.
