## Why QA/QC is a pillar of metro construction

A metro line's main structures are designed to last well over a century, operating continuously under repeated train loading while exposed to ground and groundwater conditions throughout their service life. A small defect in materials or workmanship, if not caught early, can lead to enormous repair costs or safety risk once the line is operating — at which point fixing it is rarely possible without disrupting service. For this reason, quality assurance (QA) and quality control (QC) systems are built into the construction schedule itself, not treated as a final inspection step. In Warsaw, projects expanding the Warszawskie Metro network follow QA/QC practices aligned with European (EN) standards alongside national regulations, with a clear separation between the contractor, an independent supervising engineer, and the project owner.

## Three layers of quality control

- **Incoming material inspection**: every batch of concrete, reinforcing steel, or waterproofing material must carry a mill certificate and test results from an independent laboratory before it reaches the site — concrete compressive strength, steel yield strength, membrane water permeability.
- **Process monitoring during construction**: on-site QC engineers track each construction step against an approved method statement — rebar placement accuracy, concrete curing time and temperature, and installation tolerances for tunnel lining segments or diaphragm walls.
- **Stage-by-stage sign-off (hold points)**: before work can proceed to the next step — for example, pouring concrete over a completed layer of reinforcement — the preceding stage must be inspected and formally signed off by an independent supervisor, creating an unbroken chain of records.

These three layers act as sequential hold points: a stage that fails inspection cannot proceed to the next, avoiding the far more expensive "build first, fix later" pattern that is especially costly underground.

## Quality records and traceability

Every critical component in a metro project — a tunnel lining segment, a section of diaphragm wall, a rail weld — is tagged with a unique identifier linked to its full quality record: material origin, test results, sign-off at each construction stage, and the name of the engineer who approved it. This makes it possible to trace back through a component's history if an issue arises years later; if an unusual condition is observed at a given location after years of operation, the maintenance team can look up exactly how that section was built. Digitizing these quality records, often tied into the project's BIM model, is increasingly common on European metro projects to reduce the risk of lost paper records and speed up retrieval.

## The role of independent supervision and periodic quality audits

Beyond the contractor's own QC engineers, metro projects typically engage an independent construction supervisor representing the project owner, who carries out parallel spot checks independent of the contractor's internal QC system. Periodically, a third-party quality auditor may also be brought in to assess the contractor's overall quality management system — not just individual components, but processes, staff competency, and compliance with the quality plan committed to in the contract. This multi-layered, independent oversight model reduces the conflict of interest that arises when a contractor is the sole party certifying the quality of its own work.

## Lessons for developing metro projects

Experience from Warsaw's metro projects shows that investing in a rigorous QA/QC system from the outset does not slow progress — it prevents the far more costly rework and demolition that result from defects caught too late. For metro projects still at an early stage of development, drafting a detailed quality plan as early as the tender documentation stage, clearly defining mandatory hold points and requiring independent laboratory accreditation, lays essential groundwork for structures that meet design quality standards across a service life measured in decades, if not centuries.
