## The particular challenge of a first metro project

When a city or country builds a metro system for the first time — as with the MRT Line 6 project in Dhaka, Bangladesh — the biggest obstacle is often not tunneling technology or rolling stock, but the simple fact that no local pool of engineers, supervisors, or skilled technicians has ever worked on this kind of infrastructure before. Unlike cities with several generations of metro engineers already in place, a first project has to build human capacity from scratch while still meeting international standards for schedule and quality. For this reason, technology transfer and workforce training are usually written into the EPC contract as a mandatory scope item, not an optional extra.

## Common forms of technology transfer

On a country's first metro project, technology transfer is typically organized in several layers:

- **On-the-job training**: local engineers and workers are embedded directly in crews alongside foreign experts, from operating tunneling equipment to checking concrete quality, learning by doing rather than through classroom theory alone.
- **Training abroad**: a core group of engineers is sent to a country with long-standing metro construction experience to tour active job sites, learn to operate equipment at the manufacturer's factory, or take dedicated courses in underground design and project management.
- **Resident experts**: the international contractor or consultant assigns long-term advisors to the project through construction and initial commissioning, working alongside the local team as technical mentors rather than simply directing the work.
- **Transfer of technical documentation and procedures**: design drawings, construction procedures, quality-control standards, and equipment manuals are translated and systematized for reuse on future extensions, not left as one-off paperwork for the first line alone.

## Building domestic project management capacity

Beyond construction skills, a first metro project also demands complex project management capability that the local authority usually hasn't had to exercise before: administering a large-scale EPC contract, controlling design changes, enforcing safety oversight to international standards, and coordinating multiple contractors from different countries on one site. Lenders and funding agencies often attach technical assistance components alongside financing, pairing the domestic project authority with international advisors to help it build its own management procedures for the projects that follow.

## Training the operations and maintenance workforce

Technology transfer doesn't end when construction finishes — it continues into training train operators, signaling technicians, and electromechanical maintenance staff. This is often the hardest skill set to build, since it requires deep familiarity with both the hardware and the control software, so equipment suppliers typically run simulator-based training combined with hands-on practice on the real system before the line enters commercial service.

## Retaining knowledge for the next project

A key lesson from first-time metro projects is the risk of losing institutional knowledge once the project is complete, if the trained workforce isn't retained or given a clear path to pass on what they learned to the next extension. To guard against this, many countries set up a dedicated urban rail training institute or internal research unit while the first line is still under construction, so documentation gets standardized and a core group of engineers stays in place to support the lines that follow.

## Lessons learned

The experience of first-time metro projects like Dhaka's MRT shows that effective technology transfer is not just translating technical manuals — it's a deliberately planned process tied to every phase of the project, from construction through equipment installation to commercial operation, and it works best when it's written into contract terms from the start rather than treated as a side activity. For any country preparing to build its first metro system, investing seriously in people on that very first project is what lets every subsequent line get built faster, cheaper, and with more technical self-reliance.
