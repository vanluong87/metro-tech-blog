## What is continuously welded rail?

On conventional railway and metro lines, rail is manufactured in short lengths — typically 12 to 25 meters — and joined with bolted fishplates at expansion joints that leave a small gap for the rail to expand and contract as temperature changes. Every time a wheel crosses a joint, it generates noise and impact loading, and the joint itself degrades over time.

Continuously welded rail (CWR) solves this by welding short rail sections into continuous lengths running hundreds of meters, sometimes kilometers, removing joints from most of the line. The result is a smoother ride, less noise, and significantly lower maintenance costs compared to jointed track.

## Why metro systems favor CWR

For urban metro lines — especially underground sections running close to residential buildings — controlling noise and vibration transmitted into surrounding structures is a critical engineering requirement. CWR, combined with resilient rail pads and slab track (a continuous concrete track bed replacing traditional wooden or ballasted sleepers), substantially reduces the vibration amplitude that reaches the surrounding ground. Removing joints also reduces the frequency of routine maintenance and extends the service life of the track structure.

## Welding and installation process

Creating a continuous rail section generally involves these steps:

1. **Welding in a plant or on site**: standard rail lengths are first joined using flash-butt welding, often in a factory setting, to produce longer sections that are then transported to the site; the remaining field joints are completed using thermite welding directly on the alignment.
2. **Weld quality inspection**: every weld is visually inspected, checked for dimensional accuracy, and usually subjected to non-destructive testing (ultrasonic or radiographic inspection) to rule out defects that could compromise long-term load-bearing capacity.
3. **Fastening system installation**: the rail is secured to the concrete slab or sleepers using elastic rail clips combined with resilient rubber pads, allowing a small amount of controlled movement while keeping the track geometry precisely in place.

## Controlling thermal stress and neutral temperature

The core engineering challenge of CWR is that, without expansion joints, all thermal expansion and contraction becomes internal stress along the rail — heating generates compressive stress, cooling generates tensile stress. To manage this, rail is installed and anchored at a pre-calculated "neutral temperature," usually chosen near the midpoint between the region's expected maximum and minimum temperatures. If installation happens below that neutral temperature, the rail section is mechanically stretched — using dedicated rail-tensioning equipment or localized heating — to the calculated length before final welding and anchoring, to avoid the risk of track buckling on hot summer days or rail fracture in cold winter conditions.

## Lessons from Madrid

The Madrid Metro is one of the largest and most rapidly expanded underground rail networks in Europe over recent decades. With most of its lines running underground through densely built urban areas, the system makes extensive use of slab track combined with CWR and resilient fastening systems in vibration-sensitive sections, particularly where tunnels pass close to or beneath existing buildings. This emphasis on designing noise and vibration control directly into the track structure from the outset offers a useful reference for cities expanding metro networks through dense urban fabric.

## Takeaways

CWR is far more than a simple welding technique — it is an integrated engineering system that requires precise neutral-temperature calculations, rigorous weld quality control, and a fastening system matched to the vibration-control needs of each section. Investing properly in the track installation stage pays off over the long term through lower maintenance costs, a smoother ride, and reduced impact on the surrounding environment — considerations that matter increasingly in modern urban metro projects.
