## Why interchange stations demand special design attention

In any multi-line metro network, an interchange station carries the heaviest passenger load of all, because it combines ordinary entry/exit traffic with the flow of passengers transferring between lines. A poorly designed interchange can become a bottleneck for the entire system: corridors that are too narrow, transfer walks that are too long, or crossing flows of people that collide with one another. Unlike an ordinary station, which only needs to optimize entry and exit flow, an interchange station has to be designed around forecasted transfer volumes between each specific pair of lines — not just the station's total ridership.

## Common interchange layout models

Depending on the angle at which two lines cross and the available footprint, a few layout models are used worldwide:

- **Stacked (cross-platform) interchange**: two lines cross at roughly right angles, each on its own underground level, connected by a short stair or escalator run between the two levels. This is the most common model when two lines genuinely intersect on plan.
- **Same-level interchange**: two lines running parallel or near-parallel share the same station level, so passengers transfer simply by crossing to the opposite platform — the shortest possible walk, but it requires both lines to be planned together from the start.
- **Linked stations**: when two stations were built at different times, or are operated by different entities, they may only be connected by a walking passage, sometimes with a moving walkway to help passengers cover the distance.

The same-level model is always preferred where feasible, since it minimizes transfer time, but in practice most large metro networks end up relying on stacked or linked-corridor models, because lines are typically planned and built in different phases years apart.

## Principles of passenger flow design

Good interchange design rests on a few core principles: separating boarding and alighting flows to avoid head-on crossing conflicts; providing multiple parallel transfer routes when forecasted volumes are high, rather than funneling everyone through a single pinch point; and sizing stairs and escalators based on peak-hour demand for each specific line pair, not just the station's overall throughput. Wayfinding signage and line color-coding also play a major role in letting passengers orient themselves quickly without having to stop and ask, which matters even more as the number of lines crossing at a single station grows.

## Mexico City's approach

The Mexico City Metro (Sistema de Transporte Colectivo – STC) is one of the largest and oldest metro networks in Latin America, with multiple lines crossing to form a large number of interchange stations across the system. One widely known feature of the system is its use of a unique pictogram for every station, alongside its written name — a design choice originally meant to help passengers, including those who could not read, recognize stations easily in the system's early years of operation. At the larger interchange stations linking several lines, these pictograms combine with line-specific colors to make wayfinding more intuitive, even though the transfer corridors at some of the larger stations can be fairly long, a result of local terrain and a network that expanded in stages over many years.

## Takeaways

Effective interchange design is less an architectural question than a crowd-flow modeling one: planners need to forecast transfer volumes between specific line pairs, not just total station ridership, to determine how many connecting routes are needed and where to place them. Where multiple crossing lines can be planned together from the outset, a same-level layout delivers the best possible transfer experience; where lines are built in separate phases years apart — as is common in long-established networks — long connecting corridors and clear signage become the essential compensation for what the available footprint can no longer provide.
