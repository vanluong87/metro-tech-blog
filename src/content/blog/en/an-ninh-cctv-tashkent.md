## Why metro station security needs an integrated system, not standalone cameras

A metro station is an open public space with heavy passenger flow, multiple entrances and exits, and direct connections to technical areas such as electrical rooms, signaling rooms, and pump rooms. If cameras are installed point by point without a unified surveillance architecture, the resulting footage ends up fragmented and hard to retrieve quickly during an emergency. Modern security design for metro stations therefore starts by defining monitoring zones based on risk level — concourse, platform, fare gates, technical rooms, escape routes — and then builds a camera network, transmission backbone, and operations center that function as one coherent system.

## Core components of a smart security & CCTV system

- **Risk-zoned camera network**: high-density fixed cameras at fare gates, platforms, and escape routes; pan-tilt-zoom (PTZ) cameras in larger open areas like the concourse to actively track a subject when needed.
- **Video management system (VMS)**: a centralized software platform that aggregates video feeds from an entire station — and the whole line — enabling search by time and camera location, and synchronization with other systems such as access control and alarms.
- **Smart video analytics**: algorithms that detect abandoned objects, count passengers to flag platform overcrowding, spot people moving against the flow through fare gates, or detect intrusion into restricted areas, reducing the load on manual monitoring staff.
- **Security Operations Center (SOC)**: the hub where video feeds, alerts, and communication with station staff come together, typically integrated with the operations control center (OCC) so incidents can be handled quickly and in a coordinated way.
- **Access control for technical areas**: card access or electronic locks on sensitive equipment rooms, with logged entry records linked to the camera covering each door.

## From "recording" to "smart analysis"

The first generation of CCTV systems in metro stations around the world mainly served as a record to review after the fact. More modern systems take on an active role: cameras paired with analytics software can automatically push real-time alerts to operations staff — for example, when an unusual crowd forms at one point, an object sits motionless on a platform for an abnormal length of time, or smoke or fire is detected at a very early stage. This shifts metro station security from a "react after the incident" model to one of early detection and timely intervention, which matters most in underground stations where enclosed spaces limit how much reaction time is available.

## The approach in Tashkent, Uzbekistan

The Tashkent Metro, which opened in 1977, is the first metro system in Central Asia and is well known for the distinctive public art and architecture found in many of its stations. Like many metro systems built during the Soviet era, Tashkent's stations were designed with a dual function — serving public transit while also being usable as civilian shelters in an emergency — so security and access control have always carried real weight in day-to-day operations. In recent years, the system has gradually modernized its surveillance infrastructure, adding cameras and a centralized operations center to keep pace with growing passenger volumes and contemporary urban security requirements, while preserving the architectural character of its older stations.

## Takeaways

An effective security & CCTV system for a metro station isn't measured by the number of cameras installed, but by how the resulting data is organized into a coherent architecture: zoned by risk, centralized through a VMS, and gradually upgraded with smart analytics that favor early detection over passive recording. The experience of long-running systems like Tashkent's also shows that modernizing security infrastructure can go hand in hand with preserving a station's original architectural and civilian-defense character, as long as the upgrade is planned as a coordinated effort from the design stage onward.
