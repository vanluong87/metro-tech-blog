## Why geotechnical investigation cannot be skipped

Before a metro line can be designed in detail, engineers need a clear picture of what lies beneath the alignment. Geotechnical investigation provides the foundational data for nearly every decision that follows: choosing a tunneling method, designing support structures, estimating settlement, and assessing risk to nearby buildings. Skipping or rushing this step tends to produce costly surprises and delays once unexpected ground conditions turn up during construction. Shanghai is a striking example of why this work matters, since the city sits on some of the softest and most challenging ground found in any major metro city.

## What makes Shanghai's ground conditions distinctive

Shanghai lies on a river delta, with soil deposited over thousands of years of sedimentation. Several characteristics make geotechnical investigation and risk assessment there especially important:

- **Thick layers of soft clay and mud** at shallow depth, with low bearing capacity and a tendency to deform under load or during excavation.
- **A high water table**, often just a few meters below the surface, which raises the risk of excavation wall instability and base heave if water pressure isn't carefully controlled.
- **A history of regional land subsidence** from past groundwater extraction, meaning cumulative settlement and construction-induced settlement both need careful evaluation.
- **Multiple interbedded soil layers** with varying mechanical properties at depth, requiring a sufficiently dense network of boreholes and tests to accurately characterize the ground profile along the route.

## A typical investigation and risk assessment workflow

1. **Desk study**: compiling existing geological, hydrological, and subsidence-history data for the area, along with available engineering geology maps, to flag zones that need particular attention.
2. **Field boring**: drilling a series of boreholes along the alignment and at station locations, retrieving undisturbed soil samples for laboratory testing while carrying out in-situ tests such as the Standard Penetration Test (SPT) or Cone Penetration Test (CPT) to determine bearing capacity with depth.
3. **Laboratory testing**: determining key mechanical properties such as moisture content, liquid and plastic limits, compression coefficients, and shear strength, which feed into support structure design and settlement predictions.
4. **Geotechnical risk assessment**: using the survey data, the engineering team identifies risk scenarios such as excavation wall instability, excessive settlement affecting nearby structures, or encountering unforeseen underground obstructions, and proposes corresponding mitigation measures.
5. **Setting up a monitoring program**: installing settlement, lateral displacement, and groundwater pressure sensors at sensitive locations to track conditions continuously throughout construction, allowing the construction method to be adjusted promptly if anything unusual appears.

## Lessons learned

Experience from Shanghai's metro projects shows that investing adequately in geotechnical investigation from the outset significantly reduces construction risk, particularly on soft ground with a high water table. Deploying a sufficient density of boreholes, combining multiple field and laboratory test methods, and maintaining continuous monitoring throughout the project's lifecycle are key factors that let design and construction teams respond proactively to complex ground conditions.

Overall, geotechnical investigation and pre-construction risk assessment may be quiet work that happens before a project becomes widely known, but it lays the groundwork that determines the safety and efficiency of every construction phase that follows.
