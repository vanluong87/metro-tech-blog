## Why integrated testing matters

A metro line is not simply a collection of independent systems operating side by side — it is a system of systems. Trains, signaling, traction power, ventilation, fire safety, platform screen doors, and fare collection all have to interact correctly with one another, including in abnormal situations. Each subsystem may be designed, manufactured, and tested by its own contractor to meet its own technical requirements, but that alone does not guarantee the combined system will operate safely and reliably once everything is connected. Integrated testing and commissioning is the mandatory phase — typically lasting months — that verifies exactly that, before a regulator will authorize the line to carry fare-paying passengers.

## Typical commissioning stages

Commissioning a metro line is usually organized as a sequence of increasing complexity and risk:

1. **Static testing**: each piece of equipment and each subsystem is checked individually, in place, right after installation — insulation, earthing, basic function of switchgear, sensors, and signaling equipment — with no trains running yet.
2. **Dynamic testing**: trains run on the line at gradually increasing speed and frequency, initially without passengers, to check how trains, track, signaling, and power interact under real operating conditions.
3. **Integrated testing**: multiple subsystems are tested together under coordinated scenarios — for example, verifying that a simulated fire alarm correctly triggers an emergency brake command, cuts traction power in the affected section, and opens emergency egress routes all at once — to confirm the systems "talk" to each other correctly.
4. **Degraded-mode and abnormal-scenario testing**: scenarios such as a partial power outage, a disabled train stuck mid-line, or a localized signaling fault are simulated to test response procedures and the system's ability to recover operation.
5. **Trial running**: the entire line is operated on the exact timetable planned for revenue service, continuously, for a long enough period (typically several weeks) to measure real-world reliability — such as incidents per thousand train-kilometers — before passengers are allowed on board.
6. **Limited-passenger trial / soft opening**: on some projects, before formal opening, the line is opened to a limited number of passengers or run fare-free for a short period to observe real passenger behavior at stations and on trains.

## Who is involved, and what gets checked

Integrated commissioning requires coordination among several parties: system contractors (signaling, rolling stock, power), the future operator, an independent safety assessor, and sometimes insurers as well. Safety regulators typically require a safety case — a body of evidence built up progressively through design, installation, and testing — in which integrated test results are among the most important pieces of evidence. Only once this safety case is accepted can the line be authorized to begin commercial passenger service.

## Busan's approach

Busan's urban rail network, in South Korea, combines conventional staffed metro lines with an automated, driverless light rail line, so commissioning there has to handle multiple levels of automation at once within a single interconnected network. On higher-automation lines, the volume of integrated testing is generally much greater than on conventional lines, since it must confirm that the system can correctly handle abnormal situations on its own that would previously have relied on a train operator's judgment. A common lesson from Korean urban rail networks is to build adequate time for integrated testing into the overall project schedule from the planning stage, rather than treating it as a "final check" that can be compressed when earlier construction phases run late.

## Takeaways

Integrated testing and commissioning is routinely underestimated in overall project schedules, because it happens after most of the civil construction is already finished — exactly when pressure to open the line is at its highest. Yet it is the only phase that can catch interaction failures between subsystems that no single contractor can test on its own. International experience consistently shows that compressing this phase to meet an opening date carries far greater operational risk than the benefit of opening a few weeks or months earlier.
